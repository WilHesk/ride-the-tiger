Welcome to Ride The Tiger, a brand-new series asking copywriters how they’re adapting to the new world of AI.

In this first interview, ace writer [Romey Norton](https://www.linkedin.com/in/romeynorton/) shares her optimism for the future of the industry, takes us inside the female-founded [Wordsmiths Unlimited - professional copywriting](https://www.linkedin.com/company/wordsmiths-unlimited---professional-copywriting/) … and talks about filming ghost stories with Kelsey Grammer.

She arrived at the party just as the fists started flying.

Romey Norton entered the world of copywriting in 2023, a few months after ChatGPT. Since then… well we’ve all lived it with her right?

The vanishing briefs. The existential angst. The fears of impending obsolescence. A challenging situation, to say the least. But then Romey’s had plenty of prep.

She came to copywriting from recruitment, an industry where shrinking violets get weeded out fast. She’s swum with the sharks as a movie journalist too. She’s even been in front of the camera herself, appearing in Miss Willoughby and the Haunted Bookshop, a kind of Ghostbusters-meets-Miss-Marple-meets-martial arts starring Kelsey Grammer…

Actually let’s come back to that later. Romey’s here to talk about copywriting. And she’s in positive mood, which is clearly her default setting.

“I’ve always wanted to be a copywriter,” she tells me over Zoom from her base in Yorkshire. “Previously I combined being a film journalist with my corporate life in recruitment. I always wanted to write creatively, and I just thought to myself ‘why am I doing this? I’ve got the skills and I’ve got the ability to be creative full-time.’

“I joined the industry right when people were beginning to get anxious about AI. I had lots of conversations with people hating it, and people embracing it. And I’ve really felt like this middle person, experiencing both sides and understanding both sides.

“Sure [I’ve had thoughts like] ‘oh God, am I making the wrong choice? Am I wasting my time?’ I want to be in this industry, but then you hear all this negativity. And I was thinking, ‘oh goodness, am I going to spend all this time [wanting to get in] and then in six years, we're done?’

“But no, I’m here for the long game.”

### ‘We’re forgetting it’s ok not to be creative’

Romey works at Wordsmiths Unlimited and Awards Writers. They parachute in to fill the gaps in clients’ marketing operations. Case studies, blogs, proofreading… the nuts-and-bolts stuff that keeps the marketing machine moving. And if your company is looking to win some gongs, they have a dedicated team of award-writing experts ready to turn words into wins.

Which leads us to the obvious question: how do you use AI?

It's banned at Wordsmiths. A hard, fast, under-no-circumstances-even-consider-it kind of ban. All freelancers on the team are obliged to sign a declaration that they will not use AI tools when writing.

The company does occasionally use AI for research purposes, sure. But these cases are clearly disclosed to clients. Team members check one another’s work so even the proofreading has remained resolutely human.

Only a handful of clients have asked Wordsmiths to work with AI copy. On one occasion, Romey says, “we declined because it was a case of ‘this is not us. We don’t like it.’” On another occasion, Wordsmiths trialled the ‘human in the loop’ model, checking content that’s written with AI. “It was interesting, and something we can certainly do, but we love writing things from scratch.’”

Why?

“It’s not what you want to do as a writer”, Romey says, a view that practically every copywriter on Earth can relate to right now. But it’s not just a question of personal taste.

Despite all the claims that Google doesn’t punish sloppywriting, Romey believes your Google Analytics will tell a different story.

“You're going to see whether your website is performing or not. And you’ll be able to see in your metrics [the difference between whether] you've hired somebody who's a copywriter, with or without SEO or AEO experience, or you've had Generative AI create it.

"If you rely on AI Google will hide your content. It will treat it like spam and your website will not rank.

“People forget that AI is not bespoke to your industry, your business or your culture. No matter how much you prompt it, it’s not. It’s only going to pull from something very similar that’s been prompted recently.

“A lot of companies feel like they know more because it's their business. And that’s absolutely true. You do know more. It is your business. But you are not a writer. And AI, whichever one you use, is not a writer. It's a learning tool. It's an information tool. It’s not a writing tool.

“Why people are so insistent on trying to use it for writing is beyond me. And I’m definitely seeing a shift because people are rejecting it. Especially younger audiences.

Not everybody is creative, and we’re forgetting that that’s okay. It is okay for you to not be a good writer. It's okay for you not to be an artist. But now we've given people this tool which makes them think that they are.

“I think it's going to be an exciting fad.”

### ‘The human touch is our greatest strength at the moment’

Many readers will be nodding their heads in wistful agreement here.

The deluge of bilge on LinkedIn, all the unapologetic em-dashes and hackneyed ‘not-not-just’ negations, would certainly support Romey’s assertion that AI has yet to master the writing craft.

But that will be little consolation to those who are losing their jobs to ChatGPT. Or battling to win clients in a freelance market where most gigs are getting 100 applicants a day (without even revealing the compensation).

What would Romey say to those people? Simple: be more human.

“I can absolutely appreciate that some people feel frustrated, especially if you feel that an opportunity’s taken from you. But my advice would be not to take AI personally. Go listen to ‘Video killed the Radio Star’. We’re always evolving and changing.

“If you’re finding the market tough right now, write down a little list of pros about yourself. You've got to get good at branding yourself and then being able to shout about who you are. Not just what you do, because people aren’t just buying into your service, they’re buying into you now. What can you do? Who are you as a person? Why should someone want to work with you over a free robot?

“I wrote a blog post about Ben Nevis a couple of weeks ago. I climbed it and I hated it. And I've had a couple of people message me and be like, oh, let's climb together. Oh, actually, you work in the same industry as us.’

“As a person, I can build a connection with you. I'm going to remember your kids' names. I'm going to remember that you’ve been on holiday recently. AI can only remember those details if you’ve explicitly told it. It's not going to have that same rapport that I'm going to have when I'm working with you.

“I think that human connection, that human touch that we have is our greatest strength at the moment. And if you're somebody that's really introverted and you do just like sitting behind a screen and writing, that's fine as well. There are companies that want people like that.

“You will find the people that you want to work with. And that will either be people that work with AI or people that don't. There will be jobs where you flex your creativity, there will be jobs where you don't. We get that from briefs all the time, don't we?”

And if you want to let off steam about the current job market, be sure to do it discreetly.

“People want to work with positive people" Romey continues. “You can be real, like ‘oh it’s been a hard year’. But remember there are people who love AI, people who want to work with it.

“In the long run everything’s going to be fine.”
