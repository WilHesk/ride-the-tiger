He’s the unemployed influencer whose cry for help went viral. Now after five redundancies in five years, after a battle with self-harm, panic attacks and suicidal thoughts, Joe Emery is about to cross the world for a new life.

It’s one of the most moving stories ever played out on LinkedIn. About a man who has beaten the platform by over-turning its rules, and proving that vulnerability works… despite everything you’ve read before.

___

There’s a certain irony about Joe Emery’s rise to fame.

His writing has taken off with the sort of rocket fuel most writers can only dream of.

He’s one of the most influential voices on LinkedIn. He’s done national TV, radio shows, magazines.

And it’s barely earned him a dime.

Joe started writing personally about the nightmare of doing it professionally. About the hellish job market that had made his hair fall out. Turned his teeth into bloody stumps.

About the hopelessness that had led him to contemplate taking his own life.

Even now, nearly 18 months after he snapped and blew up LinkedIn, the sums still don’t add up. All this attention has yet to translate into a meaningful job offer.

But at last there’s hope.

Joe’s got his visa for Thailand. A new start with his family, away from the UK’s cost of living crisis.

“My brother lives there,” he explains. “I won't be alone. I’ve got my sister in law and two nephews and a niece out there. It's just the cost of living that's driven it.

“So if my freelance copywriting business can work, I'm gonna go. It just depends on that working.”

### ‘I knew the job hunt wouldn’t be easy. I never thought it’d be like this’

It was all going so well too.

Back in 2023 Joe had a big gig as senior copywriter at Virgin Media O2, handling major brands like Apple, Samsung and Google.

He was scripting national campaigns, even working with Game of Thrones star Sean Bean. The kind of job us copywriters spend our whole working lives trying to get.

Then came the call.

Joe was being let go. He was one of 12% of Virgin Media O2’s staff deemed expendable. Hopes and dreams reduced to overheads and efficiencies.

Announcing the decision at the time, a company spokesperson said that any period of change "can be difficult".

They never walked a mile in Joe’s shoes.

Joe started to suffer depression and anxiety. His blood pressure skyrocketed, his hair started cascading in ashen clumps. He ground his teeth so much that he put holes in them. The panic attacks became so crippling he couldn’t breathe. And the thoughts… well, perhaps best not to go there.

This wasn’t an immediate process. No, it was a vice that kept getting tighter, as the shock of redundancy turned into jobless despair, throttling Joe's professional pride.

“It took eight months for it to really hit me,” he says now.

“I knew the job hunt wouldn’t be easy when I left Virgin Media O2. But I never guessed it would be that brutal.”

Opportunities came and were pulled away. Employers hired him for his talent, then flip-floppeed without warning. All told Joe has been laid off five times in five years, a torrent of bad luck that has never abated.

He doesn’t blame AI though. He blames the “people who see that they can cut a human out of the loop and get away with it.”

The charlatans and chancers who cheat with ChatGPT. Those cold and timid souls who see creativity as a luxury.

The people who would rather kick someone when they’re down than help them back up.

“I'm a bit sick of people just twisting a knife in my back because I'm a copywriter. Even now I get everyone telling me, ‘your career is dead, you need to stop wasting time.’ It’s like people think everyone’s job is at risk, apart from theirs.”

And LinkedIn? It was no help, this vast digital Pleasantville where triumph goes viral and misery gets ghosted. A place of gleaming eyes and pearly whites, of false humility and synthetic empathy. A place where everyone flocks to worship the wrong gods.

A place where nothing bad ever happens to anyone. Apart from you.

Eventually Joe’d had enough. So in desperation, he started to write.

This wasn’t an attempt to game the LinkedIn algorithm with feelings by numbers. Joe says he “wasn’t even thinking like a copywriter” when he picked up his phone and started typing.

“I didn’t expect it to blow up like that. I just wrote it, tossed my phone to one side and went to bed. When I woke up, it had gone bonkers.”

The engagement has barely slowed since then.

Joe’s posts have received a combined 22 million likes. He’s now got 62,000 followers, the kind of tally a tech mogul or Ted-talk titan would be proud of.

Best of all? He’s shown that in all the airbrushed, sanitised pageantry of social media, being vulnerable is actually ok.

Joe doesn’t just lean into that vulnerable side. It comes bursting out the screen. Each post is a bulletin of his fears and frustrations, starting with the most gut-wrenching. Here are some of his most recent opening lines:

“Please help me…”
“The UK broke me…”
“Unemployment stripped away my dignity…”

Then it continues. The writing is quick and sharp, keeping your attention from that first sledgehammer hook. Never letting you out of its grip.

Every line is charged with emotion. Not the tired rules of three and empty parallelisms that ChatGPT churns out. The kind that only a real human can convey.

“I just like to think [the success of his posts] is down my talent as a writer, really,” Joe says. “It's my job to tap into people's emotions. And with my posts, I tend to tap into my emotions.

“A lot of my posts, I'm not even thinking like a copywriter. I just do them from the heart.

“Pretending everything's amazing, believing all this rubbish about needing to be more positive… that’s quite damaging. Being honest is more helpful than being shamed into silence.”

Joe doesn’t just write about himself either. Many of his posts are to help fellow copywriters who are struggling for work. One [recent post](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7500160537607962624/) was dedicated to a TechOps leader whose son has a brain seizure disorder.

And he writes about writing. The craft of it. The point of it. The art of shifting from your own story towards your customers’ (the stuff that StoryBrand consultants are earning a fortune for).

But here’s another irony for you.

Those posts about copywriting? They ain’t the ones blowing up.

The ones where he writes in the first person? They’re the ones pulling in the six digits.

As Joe said on a post last week, “LinkedIn’s a funny ol’ place.”

### “I get abuse from men - always men”

Right from that first viral post last year, word spread of this LinkedIn rebel, the one who’d taken the red pill and spat out the Kool-Aid.

Joe has featured on GB News and in the Daily Telegraph. He’s also been the subject of an excellent article in [Machine](https://www.machine.news/im-struggling-unemployment-influencers-are-turning-linkedin-into-a-career/), whose editor [Jasper Hamill](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jasperhamill/) memorably called him an “unemployed influencer.”

Naturally he’s received backlash. This is the human race we’re talking about, after all.

“I get lots of abuse. ‘All you do is cry and moan, you need to man up, stop playing the victim.’ And it’s men. It’s always men.”

Does it get to him?

“No. I'm flattered… I don't know who they are, but they seem to know who I am. I also get messages from people who’ve lost family members to suicide, saying my posts have helped them.

“The nicer messages outweigh the horrible ones.”

### ‘Someone used AI to write about a death in the family’

Now Joe is ready for his new chapter.

There’s no guarantee things will be any better in Thailand. They’ve got AI there too, of course. Still, it’s a chance to turn the page on all those shitty breaks.

But the LinkedIn account? That stays.

Joe is going to keep posting. Keep sticking it to the people who think you can only win on LinkedIn by broadcasting your own success. And to the people who are using AI to write their posts for them.

“I recently saw someone post about a death in the family and the person was only young who died, but it was so AI driven. The rule of three, every word on its own line... all the tells were there.

“But more people have come out and been honest after seeing my post, so I'm glad that I've encouraged that.”

Does he have any advice for fellow LinkedInners who want to open up, but are scared of othering themselves by doing so?

“I would say go for it. People will appreciate your honesty and you never know what it might lead to. And I'd say don't let anyone shame you into silence.

“People might try and put you down, tear you down like they’ve done with me.

“Don't let them.”
