### The Setup
Romey Norton has spent over a decade writing for high-growth brands and boutique agencies. When LLMs hit the mainstream, she didn't ignore them—she brought them into her core workflow on day one. But efficiency comes with a tax.

We sat down to discuss the shift from pure writing to strategic editing, client pushback on AI-assisted billing, and how to maintain a distinct voice when algorithms want everything to sound the same.

**RIDE THE TIGER: You've been very vocal about your changing workflow. What does a typical morning look like for you now compared to two years ago?**

**ROMEY NORTON:** Two years ago, I'd stare at a blank Google Doc for an hour, agonizing over the perfect opening hook. It was a masochistic ritual. Now? I dump raw, messy thoughts, client notes, and fragmented ideas into an LLM and tell it to give me ten terrible angles. I don't use any of them, but having something to hate is the best way to figure out what I actually want to say. I haven't written a first draft in six months. It's liberating, but it also feels slightly like cheating.

**RTT: Cheating? Or just evolving?**

**RN:** Both. I think the fear is that if I'm not suffering over every word, is it still my craft? But the reality is that the market doesn't pay for my suffering. They pay for the result. The problem is that 'good enough' copy is flooding the market. My job is no longer just writing; it's protecting the brand's voice from reverting to the algorithmic mean.

**RTT: How are clients responding to this shift? Are they expecting faster turnarounds and cheaper rates?**

**RN:** That's the danger zone. I've had to be incredibly strict about boundaries. Yes, I can work faster, but that speed is the result of a decade of taste and curation. I'm not selling words by the pound; I'm selling the strategic editing and the final polish. The machines can do the heavy lifting, but they can't taste the soup. If you rely on them completely, your voice disappears. If you smash the machines, you starve.
