Punchy copy is [Martin Sayers](https://www.linkedin.com/in/martinsayers/)' calling card. That’s why he’s smashed big gigs with brands like Unilever and built a successful mentoring package for copywriters, The 10% Blueprint.

Is AI cramping his style? Not a bit of it. Actually work is better than ever. but he’s got a bone to pick with those copywriters who use AI to find the words for them.

“I have no problem if you use AI as a tool,” Martin tells us.

“But if you sell your writing, then it should be your creative input.”

This has been a regular theme of Martin’s riotously entertaining LinkedIn feed. In fact he recently set his followers a challenge: conjure a new name for the chancers and charlatans who cheat with ChatGPT.

Sloppywriters, sloppywriters… there were some good suggestions in the mix. But Martin doesn’t think they go far enough.

“If copywriters write with AI, then I see them as frauds and crooks,” he tells us now.

“Simple as that.”

### ‘I’m turning clients away now’

This isn’t a bitter rant though. Far from it.

Sure, Martin’s been a victim of an industry crisis. But it wasn’t this one.

It was the one back in 2008, when the bottom fell out of the global economy and many self-employed writers suffered as a result.

“I got myself in trouble by making the classic mistake of having one big client and few others”, he recalls.

“When that client went bust in those turbulent times, I was in trouble. But I got through it and it taught me a valuable lesson - don't keep all your eggs in one basket.”

This time? There was a bit of turbulence in 2024 when ChatGPT burst out of the research lab, wires dangling this way and that as it began laying waste to entire industries.

Martin lost a few clients. A couple even openly admitted they were choosing AI instead. But now demand is picking up again.

“I knew I just had to hold firm”, he says.

“I'm in the unusual position of having two client bases - businesses and other copywriters. I flexed more into the copywriting side of things with my courses and mentoring packages, as the need and demand was there.

“Now I'm turning many business clients away as I don't have capacity.”

I ask whether he has been tempted to drop his rate, a conundrum many writers are juggling at present.

“No,” he says, flatly.

### '‘Be frank and uncompromising’ I tell AI'

Like many of us, Martin has learned to live with our new omniscient overlord.

He uses only one AI chatbot, Claude, and admits: “I love it. It's a junior assistant I can boss around for £18 a month! It helps me by explaining complex ideas and documents in simple terms, which saves me hours of research.

“I also use it as a 'copy buddy', to read through stuff I've written and suggest changes. I always instruct it to be 'frank and uncompromising', otherwise it's too nice!

“Much of what it comes out with I disregard, but it does make the occasional good point that will lead me to rewriting a bit of copy. For example 'that headline is too broad - it needs to be more specific to your audience.'

“The other main thing I use it for is general donkey work, suggesting different words, trimming down sentence length etc.”

He doesn't write any drafts with it. “I used to experiment with this, but the output is not good and needs too much work for it to save me any time or help me.”

On balance, AI has been more a help than a hindrance. “It's made me a more prolific writer, as I can outsource the boring stuff.”

### ‘People won’t put up with this slop anymore’

Many writers will concur with that sentiment. Far from stunting our creativity, AI is giving us more time to do what we do best. The stuff our clients pay us for.

But of course there are those who will disagree.

Those who will say that AI is a bad thing period (or full stop, depending on where you’re reading). Not just for our industry but for society as a whole.

For the jobs it’s taking. For the power it’s draining. For the wealth it’s sucking out of the global economy.

For all those high-rollers who are going from six figures to zero because AI has reduced them to a red flag on a balance sheet.

And if these nay-sayers have their way? If AI is curbed, even prohibited?

Martin says he would be “happy as” if AI were abolished tomorrow. Nonetheless, when the dust settles a few years from now and the contours of our new landscape have settled, he feels we may look back and say that AI has been a force for good.

Because it’s highlighted a flaw that’s festered since humans ruled the word: A lack of creativity and an acceptance of generic mediocrity.

We used to pussy-foot around this issue when people were the culprits. Now that a robot's committing the same sin, it’s the topic of our times.

“People are saying we shouldn't put up with this slop any more - either human or robot generated”, Martin says.

“They want words from people who know what they're doing and can write copy that gets read and acted upon.

“There’s a growing number of firms, founders and entrepreneurs out there who want and need good copywriters.”

As the new working year begins, this is a sentiment we can all get behind.
