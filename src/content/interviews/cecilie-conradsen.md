This freelance superstar has written it all. Healthcare, skincare, petcare… even fish-farming (yep, really). Now she sits down with Ride The Tiger, our new series asking creative professionals how they’re adapting to the world of AI.

Things are going rather well, but she does have one question about our favourite networking platform…

“What am I even reading? What is the point of this?”

I’ve definitely thought it. You’ve almost certainly thought it. And it turns we’re not alone.

Cecilie uses LinkedIn just like we do. For the contacts, for the network, for the chance to find new clients and build her brand. And she thinks the algorithm might be… how do we say this euphemistically… not totally meritocratic.

“On LinkedIn they’re constantly saying ‘oh you have to be human, you have to tell stories’, she tells RTT. “But then the algorithm seems to reward AI slop!

“They're bringing out the slop button now. But that’s a bit ironic, isn’t it? Most of the stuff in my feed, certainly when I started really using LinkedIn, was just absolute AI.”

To be clear: This isn’t a dig at the people who didn’t send a single post until five minutes ago and are now writing with all the passion and conviction of Brené Brown on bravery.

This is about the game. Not the players.

“If someone's not a writer, I really don't have anything against them using AI to put their thoughts into words.

“But it's when you read things and you're like ‘what am I even reading? What is the point of this? This just makes no sense!’ Then they'll have 200 likes and you're like ‘why?’ That's where I'm struggling with it.

“”You’re putting a lot of effort into showing your human side but it just feels like the algorithm is a bit against you.”

### ‘Everything sounds the same’

Thankfully Cecilie came into this crazy new world with a hefty resumé in her holster.

After doing PR at university - “I swiftly realised I hated it” - she gradually worked her way up to a head of content role and has worked with major brands like Dove, Screwfix and Pedigree.

Most of her work now comes from repeat business, a testament to her client management and people skills. And a lot of her work focuses on brand development, long-form content, native emails... the sort of stuff that Claude and ChatGPT find hard to replicate.

Has she had a ‘rock-bottom’ moment with AI? “The worst bit came before I actually started trying to use AI,” she recalls. “That’s when AI really started to pick up pace.

“I had a team of copywriters that I was managing, with quite a lot of juniors. I was just so against it. I thought it would be too easy to misuse and they wouldn’t learn the art of copywriting.

“People would constantly be asking me ‘oh, are you scared AI is going to take your job?’ And because everyone kept asking, I was like ‘oh shit yeah… it actually might!’

“But when I actually started testing it out and using it I was like ‘no, it's not as good as what I can do.’

“When I first went freelance, I played around with AI to see what all the fuss was about. I gave Claude a tone of voice brief for a few different clients to see what it would come up with. And everything just sounded exactly the same.”

She uses AI for research, sure, primarily for long-form content. Blog research has gone from a two-hour slog to a near-instant process, which is particularly valuable when experts aren’t available for interview (interestingly she prefers Perplexity to Claude or Gemini because the information is usually more up to date).

It provides a useful second pair of eyes too. Like many of us Cecilie works alone, so critical feedback is hard to find.

“It’s quite nice sometimes to be able to literally just say ‘be absolutely brutal’. Some of its advice I don’t agree with, but then sometimes it does point things that I hadn’t thought of.”

But for the actual writing? Nope. Not a chance.

“Occasionally AI pops up with a line and I think ‘damn, that’s actually quite good!’ And a lot of what it produces - the parallelisms, the rule of three - comes from classic copywriting techniques. I don’t even mind the em-dash, I’ve used them for years.

“But it’s just the repetition.

"You see the same words repeated over and over. Like the word quietly - suddenly everything’s been done ‘quietly’! And AI doesn’t understand personality, emotions or humour. when it tries to be funny, it’s not funny in the slightest!”

Against that, of course, AI is fast. And it’s free.

This spells trouble for the people just entering our industry. A report posted [last month](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/indemand-consulting_marketingcareers-strategy-marketingleadership-activity-7481800411973070848--yJ_/) found that 23% of agencies are cutting junior copywriting roles, and more cuts are planned.

But the people who’ve been doing it for years? The ones who’ve shown their value and have the results to prove it? There’s still a market.

“It must be an absolute nightmare for new starters,” Cecilie says. “I really feel for those starting out.”

“But I don’t think AI is ever going to [match an experienced human writer]. Well, you never know, perhaps it will one day, but hopefully that’s past my time!”

### ‘I’m quite introverted - but what’s the worst that can happen?’

As we enter the busy autumn period Cecilie’s diary is pretty full. However like any good freelancer, she’s always on the lookout for new biz. And LinkedIn is becoming an ever-bigger source of leads - despite that questionable algorithm.

“It's always the way isn't it? You put more time into client branding than your own, and your own marketing falls by the wayside. But recently I’ve really been focusing on self-branding, particularly on LinkedIn.”

This hasn’t come easily, she explains. As a natural introvert who “hated answering questions in front of the class at school”, her struggle is one that many writers can relate to.

Putting out a post on LinkedIn, and risking the avalanche of tumbleweed, can feel daunting for all of us. Particularly with all those mega-liked posts plunging an ice axe into our self-esteem.

But it’s a necessary evil, part of the modern writer’s lot.

“I’ve just had to suck it up and try to remove my ego from the process”, Cecilie says. “The imposter syndrome is real, but then you'll get a connection request or a message from someone saying your post really resonated with them, and it lifts you up again.

“It's a bit of a rollercoaster, for sure, and you definitely get that same feeling you got when you raised your hand at school. But you just have to think: what's the worst that can happen vs what's the best thing that can happen?

“Worst thing - no one likes your post. Annoying, yes, but not the end of the world.

“Best thing - you get a new client or make a new connection off the back of it.”

So, what advice would she give to any LinkedIn rookies? “Be consistent. The more you put in, the more you get out. Follow relevant people with relevant job titles and you might strike gold.”

Even if you’ve got to sift through all those nonsense posts to get there.
